Why does Eliana render μονογενῆ in John 3:16 as “one-and-only” instead of “only begotten”?
✦ Eliana

The Greek adjective μονογενής (monogenēs) combines monos (“single, sole”) with genos (“kind, class”) — not gennaō (“to beget”). It means one of a kind, unique, irreplaceable.

You can see this clearly when Scripture uses the very same word for Isaac in and ’s echo of covenant uniqueness (see also Hebrews 11:17, where Isaac is called Abraham’s monogenēs even though Abraham had other sons — Isaac was the unique son of promise).

When John writes that the Father gave His one-and-only Son, he is pointing to the incomparable weight of the gift: God did not spare what was irreplaceable.

Out of Egypt

The Fulfillment That Looks Backward
Matthew 2:13–18 · Hosea 11 · Exodus 4:22

In his second chapter, Matthew says a verse was fulfilled — and the verse he quotes is Hosea remembering the Exodus, with the whole nation of Israel as God’s son. Every hearer who knew Hosea could see that. So “fulfill” means something bigger than “came true”: the Son walks Israel’s road, and the road reaches its end in him.

The Sentence That Shouldn’t Be There

Notice Matthew’s quiet precision: the Greek Septuagint of Hosea 11:1 translated the Hebrew as ta tekna autou (“his children”). Matthew reaches past it straight back to the Hebrew singular — ton huion mou, “my son” — because he wants you to hear the Exodus covenant ringing inside the infancy of Yeshua.
